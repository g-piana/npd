This deserves a straight answer, because the 160-emails-zero-replies experience contains the whole lesson — it just needs decoding. Let me work through it using your own frameworks, since your repo already contains most of the answer applied to a different product.

Diagnosis first: you weren't rejected on the product, you were rejected on the category. Run the Anomaly Inspector outreach through Moesta's four forces. Push: an SME plant manager doesn't wake up feeling the cost of defects — it's amortized into scrap rates and warranty budgets he's lived with for twenty years. No push. Pull: "AI visual inspection" sounds like a project, not a purchase. Anxiety: high — new tech, unclear integration, "will this make me look stupid." Habit: maximum — the human inspector is already paid for. Three forces against you, zero for you, delivered through the coldest possible channel. Zero replies is the predicted outcome, not a mystery. It says almost nothing about whether the product is good — and, importantly, almost nothing about whether a different route works.

The second lesson: never market the technique. "King of Conformal Prediction" is positioning for exactly two audiences: investors evaluating your moat, and hiring. For buyers, the technology belongs in the engine room. Nobody bought "relational databases"; they bought Oracle so the bank's ledger balanced. Your STYLE.md already codifies this instinct — one idea per sentence, plain language, the honest number. The marketing question is never "how do I explain neural operators" but "what outcome do I name that a plant manager already wants." For idea #5, that outcome has a name, and it's not predictive maintenance.

It's false alarms. Here's the positioning insight I'd build everything on: your best early market is not manufacturers who've never tried predictive maintenance — it's the ones who tried it and got burned. Data-driven PdM has a well-known, deeply felt failure mode: alert floods, alarm fatigue, technicians chasing anomalies that turn out to be a hot day or a load change, then everyone quietly ignoring the dashboard. Those buyers have push (existing pain), low anxiety (they already believe in the category), budget precedent (they've spent money on this), and a crisp evaluation question ("does this stop crying wolf?"). The physics angle translates perfectly into their language: the system knows whether the temperature is expected given the load — so when it alarms, it means it. You're not creating a category; you're fixing a broken one. Dunford would call this repositioning within an existing frame, which is 10x cheaper than category creation for a solo founder.

Now the channel question — and here I'd push back gently on "bigger software companies." Selling the physics layer to PdM software vendors is possible but structurally weak: they're simultaneously your customer and your future competitor, and a single physicist hire erodes you. The channel that actually fits idea #5 is equipment OEMs — the compressor maker, the gearbox maker, the heat-exchanger manufacturer. Look at why this works structurally: your idea card's biggest problem is that each application needs its own physics model. Sold direct to SMEs, that's a cost you re-incur per deal. Sold to an OEM, one physics model covers their entire machine family across their whole installed base — the economics invert from your weakness into your unit of scale. The OEM also solves your kill-criterion risk that SMEs lack sensors: new machines ship instrumented. And OEMs have their own "why now": servitization pressure (selling uptime contracts, where false alarms cost them money) plus CRA and Data Act obligations landing on exactly their connected products — which means your compliance-adjacent credibility from the CRA work compounds rather than resets. One OEM deal is ten thousand SME cold emails.

On packaging: yes, hide everything, but understand what packaging can and can't do. The product surface should be brutally simple — expected vs. actual, one delta, one alarm that's trustworthy, maybe a "physics check" badge an operator learns to trust. But packaging fixes comprehension, not distribution. Beautiful packaging cold-emailed to 160 SMEs gets 160 non-replies with nicer screenshots. Packaging matters at the moment of evaluation; the channel and the felt pain get you to that moment.

For awareness, you already wrote the playbook — for CRA. Be useful in public first, peer frame, teardown posts with the honest number. Port it directly. Your RE:MARK sim-to-real teardowns are structurally the exact content this needs: take a public PdM claim ("94% failure prediction accuracy"), show the honest number (false positive rate per machine per month, which nobody publishes), explain the mechanism plainly (correlation models can't distinguish "hot" from "hotter than physics allows"), give three checkable questions, close without moralizing. Then go one step further than you did with CRA: demonstrate, don't describe. Take a public dataset — wind turbine SCADA data is openly available — and publish a side-by-side: data-driven detector vs. physics-informed detector, same signals, count the false alarms. For a complex technology, one honest public demo does what fifty explainer posts can't, and it's simultaneously your Mom Test magnet: the people who comment "this is exactly our problem" are your interview pipeline, pre-qualified.

So the sequence I'd suggest, consistent with keeping validation first: interview OEM product managers and PdM-disappointed maintenance leads (not PdM virgins), asking Mom Test questions about their last alert flood and what it cost; run the teardown/demo content engine in parallel to fill that interview pipeline; and pick the beachhead machine class — your idea card's open question — based on which OEM segment shows the most servitization pressure. The positioning that falls out is something like: monitoring that knows what your machine should be doing — physics never mentioned, physics doing all the work underneath.

One honest caveat: I'm reasoning from pattern and your own documented evidence, and the OEM hypothesis is exactly that — a hypothesis. It has its own risks (long sales cycles, pilots that stall in innovation departments) and deserves the same Mom Test treatment before you commit the physics-model investment to any one machine family.
