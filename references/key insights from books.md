### The six books — actionable core of each

**1. Fitzpatrick, _The Mom Test_**

The one insight: people will lie to you about whether they'd buy your product — not maliciously, but out of politeness. So never ask "would you use this?" or "do you think this is a good idea?" Instead, ask about their _past behavior and current pain_. Three rules: talk about their life, not your idea; ask about specifics in the past, not hypotheticals about the future; talk less, listen more.

Actionable technique: In every interview, ask "What do you currently do when [problem] happens?" and "How much time/money does that cost you?" and "When was the last time this happened?" If they can't answer concretely, the problem isn't real enough to pay for. Never pitch your solution during a problem interview.

**2. Moesta, _Demand-Side Sales 101_**

The one insight: people don't buy products, they "hire" them to make progress in their life. And the decision to buy is driven by four forces — two pushing toward change (the push of the current situation being bad, and the pull of the new solution being attractive) and two pulling against change (the anxiety of the new solution, and the inertia/habit of the current way). Most founders focus only on making their product attractive (pull) and ignore the other three forces, which is why good products don't sell.

Actionable technique: In interviews, map all four forces. "What was happening when you first started looking for a solution?" (push). "What attracted you to [competitor/workaround]?" (pull). "What made you hesitate?" (anxiety). "What kept you doing it the old way for so long?" (habit). The strongest opportunities have high push (regulation deadline, fine, audit failure) and low habit (no entrenched incumbent).

**3. Dunford, _Obviously Awesome_**

The one insight: positioning isn't a tagline, it's a deliberate choice of competitive context. The same product can be positioned as "a better spreadsheet," "a lightweight database," or "a compliance management tool" — and each framing changes who buys it, what they compare it to, what they'll pay, and whether they see your strengths as relevant. Most founders position by instinct and end up competing against the wrong alternatives.

Actionable technique: Run this five-step sequence for each idea. (1) List your competitive alternatives — what does the buyer use today? (2) List your unique attributes versus those alternatives. (3) Ask what _value_ each attribute delivers to the buyer. (4) Ask which buyer segments care most about that value. (5) Pick the market context that makes your value obvious. If step 1 is "nothing" or "Excel," that's usually good — it means no real competitor, and your bar to clear is low.

**4. Torres, _Continuous Discovery Habits_**

The one insight: discovery (talking to customers, testing assumptions) should be a weekly habit, not a phase. Most teams do a burst of interviews, then build for months in a cave, then discover they built the wrong thing. Torres proposes an "opportunity solution tree" — a visual map that connects a desired outcome to opportunities (unmet needs) to solutions to assumption tests.

Actionable technique: The opportunity solution tree. Draw your desired outcome at the top ("Companies managing CRA compliance"). Branch into opportunities (unmet needs you've heard in interviews). Branch each opportunity into possible solutions. For each solution, identify the riskiest assumption and design the smallest possible test. Do this weekly, not as a one-off.

**5. Sarasvathy, _Effectuation_**

The one insight: expert entrepreneurs don't start with a goal and work backward (causal reasoning). They start with their means — who they are, what they know, whom they know — and discover opportunities by combining those means in unexpected ways. They also prefer partnerships over competitive analysis, affordable loss over expected return, and leveraging surprises rather than avoiding them.

This is already embedded in our process (Stage 1 is literally an asset inventory). You've absorbed the actionable core.

**6. Ries, _The Lean Startup_**

The one insight: build the smallest thing that lets you test your riskiest assumption, measure whether it worked, learn, and iterate. The "MVP" concept. This has been so widely absorbed into startup culture that you almost certainly already practice it. The book itself is mostly anecdotes from 2008-era startups.
