**1. Be useful in public first, ask for nothing (highest yield, 2-3 weeks to payoff)**

Write a short, genuinely useful piece — not a blog post about your product (which doesn't exist), but a practical guide: "CRA Article 14 reporting: what SME manufacturers actually need to do by September 11." Keep it to 800 words, specific, no fluff, no sales pitch. Post it on LinkedIn, share it in the Industrial Cyber community, post it on relevant subreddits. End with: "I'm researching how manufacturers of connected products are preparing for this. If this is on your radar, I'd be curious to hear how you're approaching it."

This inverts the dynamic. You're not asking for time — you're giving value and inviting conversation. The people who respond are self-selecting: they have the problem, they're thinking about it, and they're reaching out to you. You're also building a public artifact that compounds — every person who shares it brings more potential interviewees.

**2. Go where the anxiety is already being expressed (immediate)**

Don't create conversations — join existing ones. Specific places where CRA anxiety is surfacing right now:

- **LinkedIn**: search for posts containing "CRA September" or "Cyber Resilience Act reporting" or "SBOM compliance." Look for people asking questions or sharing concerns — they've publicly signaled they're dealing with this. Comment with something useful (not "let's chat"), then DM with a specific, relevant observation about their situation.
- **Industrial Cyber community**: you already follow this feed. Find discussion threads about CRA readiness.
- **ENISA webinars and events**: ENISA is running SRP onboarding sessions before September. Attend. The attendees are exactly your target — manufacturers trying to figure out the reporting process. The networking during and after these events is natural, not cold.
- **Reddit r/cybersecurity, r/embedded, r/IoT**: search for CRA-related threads. People asking questions there are practitioners, not executives — but practitioners are often better interview subjects because they describe the actual workflow, not the corporate narrative.

**3. The peer frame, not the researcher frame (for direct outreach)**

When you do reach out directly, the framing matters enormously. Compare these two messages:

_Bad:_ "Hi, I'm researching CRA compliance challenges for connected product manufacturers. Could I have 15 minutes of your time to discuss your experience?"

This reads as: you want something from me, you'll probably pitch me, and "15 minutes" always means 30.

_Better:_ "Hi [name], I saw your post about [specific thing they said or did]. I'm a physicist turned engineer who's been working on SBOM tooling — and I'm honestly trying to figure out whether this September deadline is actually going to bite SME manufacturers or if everyone will just wing it. You seem to be right in the middle of this. I'd genuinely value your perspective — happy to share what I'm hearing from others in return."

What's different: you're a peer with relevant expertise (not a "researcher"), you're referencing something specific they did (not a template), you're asking a genuine question you're curious about (not a disguised sales call), and you're offering reciprocity (sharing what others are doing — this is valuable to them because they're also wondering what their peers are doing).

**4. The "who else should I talk to?" chain (for building network from scratch)**

You don't have a network in IoT/edge manufacturing. But you need only one entry point. Find one person — through any of the methods above — and at the end of the conversation ask: "This has been really helpful. Is there anyone else you know who's dealing with the same CRA preparation challenge? I'd be curious to compare approaches across different types of products."

People are much more willing to introduce you to a peer than to give you their own time. And the introduction carries implicit endorsement: "My colleague Giulio is researching CRA readiness, he's technical, the conversation was useful, you should talk to him." One good interview can generate 2-3 warm intros, which each generate 2-3 more. Five interviews become achievable from a single entry point.

**5. Italian industry associations (home market advantage)**

You're in Italy. Italy transposed NIS2 early and Italian manufacturers of connected products face the same CRA obligations. Look for:

- **Confindustria Digitale** — the digital industry branch. They likely run working groups on CRA/NIS2 compliance.
- **ANIE Automazione** — the Italian association for automation, robotics, and mechatronics manufacturers. Their members are exactly in CRA scope.
- **Local Confindustria chapters in Lombardy** — they host events, roundtables. Attending one and asking "how are your members preparing for CRA?" in person is 10x more effective than any LinkedIn message.

These associations exist to help their members navigate regulation. If you position yourself as someone researching the problem (truthfully), they may even help you reach their members directly.
